Case study · O3

Rebuilding a team while it was still running.

I joined O3 at the height of the Great Resignation. So I paused the backfill and built the system first: what the job is, how to grow, and how we know it's working.

Organization
O3, a 40+ person digital experience consultancy
My role
Director to SVP to Chief Experience Officer. Senior leadership team.
Scope
Org design, career architecture, leadership development, goals + performance

The headline numbers

Less guessing. More growing.

76% → 88.2%

Client satisfaction, after we rebuilt how the team worked across disciplines.

13

Roles across research, design, and writing, on one ladder with two ways up.

50+

Practice skills anyone could self-assess, from "never done this" to "I could teach it."

The story

Hiring fast would have rebuilt the same problem.

01 / 04 · Context

Where O3 stood.

I joined in October 2020. The team was turning over, the hiring market was brutal, and nothing about how the design function worked was written down. Expectations lived in people's heads, and people were leaving with them.

02 / 04 · The challenge

Everyone had a different definition of good.

Designers couldn't tell you what "senior" meant here. Managers had no shared language for feedback. Clients got great work from some teams and uneven work from others. Filling seats fast would have locked all of that in.

03 / 04 · The approach

Define the team, then hire it.

I started with the business: what clients bought from us, where delivery broke down, and which skills we'd need in three years. Then we built four connected pieces, each one feeding the next. The charter said what we do. The skills matrix said who does it and how they grow. Leader lanes turned leadership into owned work. The goals framework tied all of it to company, department, and individual goals.

04 / 04 · My role

Director, SVP, then CXO.

I joined as director of the Experience Team, moved up to SVP, and finished as Chief Experience Officer, on the senior leadership team the whole way. I authored the frameworks, wrote the charter with my team, ran everything on our own work first, then took it firm-wide through my leadership lane.

The operating system

Four pieces. One shared language.

1

Team charter

One document for how we work, written with the team. Nine chapters, from a north star to what "done well" means.

  • A team blueprint and ten core values
  • Ground rules and day-to-day norms
  • Standards for creating, presenting, and measuring work
2

Skills matrix

Thirteen roles across research, design, and writing, each defined by scope, consulting skill, supervision, leadership, and AI fluency.

  • A Y-shaped path: lead as an expert or lead through people
  • Company values written into every level
  • 50+ practice skills, self-assessed past and present
3

Leader lanes

Each senior leader owned a lane of the business. Mine was experience. Every tactic had a milestone, budget, timeline, and two owners: one from leadership, one from staff.

  • Managers and project leads co-owned real initiatives
  • Leadership practiced on live work, beyond the org chart
4

Goals framework

Year one defined the frameworks. Year two turned them into shareable action plans inside company, department, and individual goals.

  • Annual planning with SWOT and a three-year view
  • Reviews measured against the same language people grew against

When we get it right, it's because we got it wrong first.

Try the scale

Growth you could describe out loud.

Every practice skill in the matrix used the same five-step self-assessment. Tap a level.

I've done this successfully several times, and I feel comfortable teaching it to other people.

Climb the ladder

Every role, spelled out.

Pick a track and a level. This is the real matrix, lightly edited for the web.

One lane, up close

Experience: three initiatives, two years.

1

Delivering excellence

A consistent, polished approach to every client interaction. We co-wrote a definition of excellence for each stage of a project, mapped every CX and UX activity to our solutions from pitch to delivery, and packaged workshops into templates and training.

2

Nurturing loyalty

A framework to recognize and reward long-term client relationships: launches and anniversaries, lunch and learns, roundtables, and client dinners.

3

Measuring relationships

A regular read on project and relationship health: a client benchmark survey twice a year, project surveys, documented retros, and quarterly business reviews for long-term partners.

→

Define, then deploy

2022 was for defining the frameworks and planning the resources behind them. 2023 was for deploying them as action plans inside company, department, and individual goals.

The toolkit

Seven templates. Every engagement starts the same way.

Excellence stopped depending on who was staffed. Each service got a ready-to-run Miro template, so a first-year designer and a principal ran the same playbook.

  1. Project kickoffTeam roles, meetings, milestones, timeline, and risk + change management
  2. Discovery workshopBusiness goals, rose, bud + thorn, affinity clustering, and prioritized recommendations
  3. Naming workshopTone + traits, mind map, word bank, and a scored ranking
  4. Visual design workshopBrand attributes, color, type, photography, and iconography directions
  5. Landscape analysisCompetitors, inspiration, findings, and what we recommend
  6. Research readoutGoals, analysis, summary, and next steps
  7. Account healthMid- and end-of-project sentiment, retros, QBR content, and growth ideas

Results

A team that knew what good looked like.

  • Rebuilt the Experience Team with people hired against a clear vision and a clear ladder.
  • Client satisfaction rose from 76% to 88.2% through cross-functional process changes.
  • The leadership frameworks spread firm-wide, well beyond the design team.
  • AI fluency became a stated expectation at every level, years before most teams wrote it down.
